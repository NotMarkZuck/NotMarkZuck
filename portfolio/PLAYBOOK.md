# Portfolio Playbook — Jeremi Lukos, M.Arch applications (MIT · Harvard GSD · Columbia GSAPP)

A working reference for the makeover. Part 1 is the hard rules, taken from each school's own admissions pages (checked October 2026). Part 2 is what reviewers reward, with sources. Part 3 is the audit of the current portfolio. Part 4 is the identity strategy. Part 5 is the makeover checklist.

Sources: [MIT Graduate Admissions](https://architecture.mit.edu/graduate-admissions) · [GSD M.Arch I requirements](https://www.gsd.harvard.edu/architecture/programs/master-of-architecture-i/application-requirements-master-of-architecture-i/) · [GSAPP requirements](https://www.arch.columbia.edu/admissions/requirements)

### Scholarships — what "with scholarship" actually means
- **Columbia:** incoming-student scholarships are "awarded based on recommendations by the program's admissions committee." There is no separate application, so the portfolio *is* the scholarship application. ([GSAPP scholarships](https://www.arch.columbia.edu/admissions/tuition-aid/scholarships))
- **Harvard GSD:** most aid is need-based and assigned by formula. The big merit awards (e.g. the Cossutta full-tuition fund) are given *after* the core studios. ([GSD M.Arch aid](https://www.gsd.harvard.edu/resources/endowed-fellowships-financial-aid/march-financial-aid))
- **MIT:** fellowships are described as need-based, up to full funding.
- → At GSD and MIT, a top portfolio mainly wins **admission**, and money follows need. At Columbia, being at the very top of the pool is what earns merit money.

## Part 2 — What reviewers reward (evidence)

1. **Process over product.** UVA's guidelines encourage "the iterative steps of design development that display your thought process, and not just the 'final' products." CCNY says committees "would rather see the process … than only the final result." An admitted MIT applicant's (Alek Dojnow's) portfolio was built around prototypes → tests → critiques. ([UVA](https://www.arch.virginia.edu/sites/default/files/2023-12/Graduate-Programs-Portfolio-Guidelines-2024_v2.pdf), [CCNY](https://ssa.ccny.cuny.edu/admissions/graduate/graduate-application-portfolio-tips/), [ArchPrep – MIT](https://architectureprep.com/2023/03/07/mit-student-reviews-accepted-architecture-school-portfolio-march/))
2. **Fewer, deeper projects.** The admitted MIT applicant used **two** projects and cut weaker work. His regret was not including "projects from outside academia — things that have personal meaning to me."
3. **Images carry the story; text is minimal.** The pages must be readable without the paragraphs. "Admissions committees at these schools are not going to spend time reading your descriptions." Use a clear hierarchy: a few large images, the rest small. (Advice from [Archinect forum](https://archinect.com/forum/thread/150285720/advice-on-a-successful-portfolio-applying-for-march-1-harvard-gsd-yale-yosa-and-columbia-gsapp) users; anonymous but consistent.)
4. **Content over graphic styling.** "GSD and YSOA are looking more at content and project concepts than abstruse graphics"; "too much graphic design than the design itself" is a named failure.
5. **Honest authorship.** Role stated on group work ([ArchPrep – GSD](https://architectureprep.com/2023/03/27/harvard-gsd-student-reviews-her-accepted-architecture-school-portfolio-march/)). AI imagery is a live authenticity worry in design-school admissions ([Artnet](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/art-schools-and-artificial-intelligence-2377262?amp=1)), and UK architecture schools name "process over product … drawing as self-expression" as values under pressure from AI ([RIBA on SCHOSA report](https://www.riba.org/work/insights-and-resources/professional-features/ai-professional-features/what-can-architects-learn-from-an-ai-in-education-report/)).
6. **Tailor the final section per school.** The admitted GSD applicant changed her last section for each school.
7. **3D thinking at any scale.** Both MIT and GSD say this explicitly. Show it with sections, axonometrics, models and joints, not just exterior renders.

### The schools' cultures (my synthesis — use it to choose emphasis, not to imitate)
- **MIT:** making, technology, computation, research, and architecture's social reach ("those it serves"). Your precast logistics work, physical prototypes and data skills belong here.
- **Harvard GSD:** conceptual clarity, rigorous drawing, individual authorship, the landscape/urban relationship. Your "listening to land" and site work belong here.
- **Columbia GSAPP:** critical, experimental, an architect's position on the world, cities, NYC. A strong idea about the city and its stories belongs here.

## Part 4 — Identity strategy: what would excite a reviewer

Don't invent a persona. Arrange the evidence you already have around **one through-line**:

> **Architecture as listening.** Before designing, Jeremi listens to a place: its ground, its data, its people, its materials. The building then tells that story back.

The parts of you that support it:
1. **The Listener-Storyteller** (introvert, tour guide). Show it through *site listening* drawings: sound/walk maps, hand-drawn observations, interviews, story-maps. One or two new pieces made for the portfolio would be enough.
2. **The Economist.** "Listening" with data: R/Stata analyses of who a building serves, cost and logistics. The precast truck-trip study already does this. Make it a signature graphic language, not a stray chart.
3. **The Maker.** Models in hands, joints, moulds, prototypes. Evidence of real "3D thinking."
4. **The Observer-Traveller.** Hand sketches from India, Germany and Spain. Use them *inside* projects as the first act of listening, not as an appendix.
5. **The Multilingual / Kodaikanal upbringing.** Mainly for the statements. In the portfolio, a single personal project or sketch series rooted in that landscape could be the "outside academia" piece the MIT applicant wished he had included.

Test for every page: *Does this show how I perceive or structure the world, or just what a building looks like?*

## Part 5 — Makeover checklist

**Structure (≤ 20 PDF pages, counting each spread as one page):**
- 1 cover (name, program, a meaningful image; no word "PORTFOLIO")
- 1 contents / short intro spread with the thesis in ≤ 40 words, a table of projects with dates, type and role
- **GS Gallery** (individual) — ~5 spreads: listening to the site → concept → sections and plans → precast system and logistics → model
- **Mountain Library** (team, role stated) — ~4 spreads: site and data → canopy prototype → plans/sections → the hand-held model and the façade
- **Beer Garden or The Loop** — keep **one**, ~3 spreads, with real drawings (plan, section). Merge or cut the other.
- **A personal / outside-academia piece** — ~2 spreads (sketch series, travel observation, data-story, or something from home)
- 1 closing page
- (MIT only: up to 10 extra pages for an MIT-specific appendix, e.g. making/data research)

**Rules:**
- Lead each project with an idea image (sketch, diagram, model), not a render.
- At most 1 render per project, one you made yourself; drop all AI-generated imagery.
- Each project needs at least one orthographic drawing (plan/section) and one 3D drawing (axon/model).
- Captions ≤ 2 lines; one ≤ 60-word idea statement per project.
- Remove software tags and skill adjectives.
- Label every project: semester/year · academic/office/personal · individual/team (+ role) · instructor.
- One typeface family, a consistent grid, real page numbers, headers that never sit on top of images.
- Spell-check twice; get a native-English reader to proofread.
- Export the screen PDF under 32 MB; check how it reads on a laptop at 100% zoom.
